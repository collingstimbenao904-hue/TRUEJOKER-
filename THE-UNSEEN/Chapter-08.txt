

THE UNSEEN

 Chapter 8: The Teacher's Question

Saturday morning arrived quietly.

Ntokozo woke up earlier than usual, washed his face, and prepared for the meeting with his teacher.

He wasn't sure why he had been asked to come to school. He kept thinking about the previous day's conversation.

Had someone complained about him?

Had he done something wrong?

Or was there something else?

After completing his morning chores, he told his guardian that he was going to school.

"Don't waste the whole day there," the guardian replied.

Ntokozo nodded and left.

When he arrived, the school looked different without the usual noise of students. The classrooms were quiet, and the football field was empty.

He walked towards the staffroom and knocked.

"Come in," a voice answered.

It was Mr. Dlamini, his class teacher.

"Good morning, sir."

"Good morning, Ntokozo. Have a seat."

Ntokozo sat down.

Mr. Dlamini placed a few exercise books on his desk and looked at him.

"How are you doing at school?"

"I'm fine, sir."

"How are your studies?"

"Everything is okay."

The teacher paused.

"Ntokozo, I have been teaching you for some time. I've noticed that you're a bright boy, but you rarely participate in class. You hardly speak to anyone, and you always seem to be alone."

Ntokozo lowered his eyes.

"Is something troubling you?"

"No, sir."

Mr. Dlamini leaned back in his chair.

"Listen, I didn't call you here to punish you. I called you because I want to understand you."

Ntokozo remained silent.

The teacher continued.

"Sometimes, the students who say the least are the ones who need someone to listen to them the most."

For a moment, Ntokozo felt something strange.

Someone had noticed.

Not his old shoes. Not his quietness. Not his position at the back of the classroom.

Someone had noticed him.

"Sir," Ntokozo finally said.

"Yes?"

"Can I ask you something?"

"Of course."

"Does being alone mean there's something wrong with you?"

Mr. Dlamini looked at him carefully.

"No, Ntokozo. Having no friends doesn't make you a bad person. And you should never believe that you're less important because other people fail to see your worth."

Ntokozo swallowed.

He wanted to say more, but the words wouldn't come.

Before leaving, Mr. Dlamini gave him a small notebook.

"I want you to write down your thoughts. Your feelings, your dreams, even the things you find difficult to explain. You don't have to show anyone unless you want to."

Ntokozo accepted it.

"Thank you, sir."

As he walked home, he held the notebook tightly.

For once, he wasn't thinking about the people who ignored him.

He was thinking about the person who had taken the time to ask whether he was okay.

That evening, he opened the new notebook and wrote its first sentence:

"Maybe being unseen doesn't mean nobody can ever see you. Maybe it simply means you haven't met the right people yet."

He smiled.

But somewhere inside him, a question remained.

Would people still see him if they knew everything?
